Criminals build near-perfect copies of well-known Czech shops' websites, and the copied shop is left with thousands of complaints [S2].

- Sportisimo, Tescoma and Super zoo were all copied in one 2025 wave [S2].
- 7,500 fake Czech shops were counted over a single Christmas [S1].
- Several copies of a known retailer can appear and vanish in a day [S3].

The customers who lose money were never the copied shop's to begin with, but the anger arrives at its support desk anyway [S2].

- Fraud with fake Czech shops rose 1,190% year on year in the first quarter of 2025 [S1].
- Most of the fakes counted that Christmas were later taken down or moved to a new address [S1].
- The copies use the real shop's logo, design and contact details, and advertise 70% to 90% off everything [S2,S3].
- Facebook and TikTok posts are the main way the copies reach shoppers [S4].
- A big Czech perfume retailer says it meets attacks like these daily across Europe [S4].

Existing non-solutions: No Czech firm sells this work; what is sold here is register watching, inspection services, film-industry enforcement and lawyers by the case [S13].

- Czech trademark watchers check the register of new filings, not copied sites [S13].
- Czech law firms sell warning letters, injunctions and domain disputes case by case [S5].
- One international platform markets this here with no Czech office or site [S13].

The state's answer is aimed at the shopper, not the brand. The Czech Trade Inspection Authority publishes a list of e-shops it treats as risky, and its own page says that putting a site on the list is only a recommendation [S13]. A global inspection group sells unauthorised-seller detection inside supply-chain work, and a film-industry association has illegal copies deleted for its members, not for shops [S13]. The rows are under [Market gap](#competition).

Why now: Copies of known Czech shops keep coming back under new addresses, and the shops that field the complaints cannot stop them any faster [S1,S2].

- The copied shop's support desk absorbs thousands of refund demands [S2].
- A copy taken down moves to a new address and starts again [S1].
- Seven Czech shops and carriers had to club together against this in 2025 [S4].

No law puts a date on any of it. The dates that do exist are these:

- In April 2025 an antivirus firm reported 1,190% more fake-shop fraud in Czechia year on year, with 7,500 fake Czech shops counted over one Christmas [S1].
- In September 2025 the trade ministry warned that several copies of a well-known retailer can appear and disappear in a single day [S3].
- In September 2025 the copied shops and the retail association reported the fakes to the trade inspectors, Facebook, the domain operators and the police, and weeks later nothing had happened [S2].
- In November 2025 seven Czech shops and carriers joined one campaign against people impersonating their brands [S4].
- In March 2026 a Prague law firm published a guide to fighting copycats, and updated it that June [S5].

Who pays: Czech shops pay lawyers and the arbitration court case by case, and no Czech seller prices the watching and the removing [S13].

- Czech law firms bill this work case by case [S5].
- What carries a published Czech price is the legal step, not the watching [S13].
- No Czech vendor publishes a price for finding and removing one copy [S13].

Both figures on file are asking prices from a published list, not sums any shop is known to have paid; they are under [Willing to pay](#willing-to-pay). A search of the public contracts register for brand-protection wording returned nothing usable, because its web form builds its rows by script, so it proves nothing either way [S13].

Solved elsewhere: Brands in Germany, Spain, Lithuania and the United States already buy software that finds the copies and files the removals [S8,S9].

The German seller watches over 120 marketplaces and social networks for a certification body that names it publicly, and files the takedowns automatically [S9]. The Spanish seller has customers in more than 25 countries and has raised over 20 million euros [S11]. The Lithuanian seller, which raised its first round in September 2026, says it removes more than 33,500 infringements a month for over 20 customers [S8]. The rows are under [Validated abroad](#validated-abroad).

## First moves

1. Build a daily watcher for new web addresses and marketplace listings using one Czech shop's brand name, and show it to that shop free. Take it to the person who runs their online store: the shops copied in the last waves already know the problem, and the complaints land on their own support desk, as [The opportunity](#opportunity) shows. Start with one brand, one alerting email, and a link to the evidence.
2. Call the person who answers customer complaints at a mid-size Czech shop and ask what a copied site costs them in a week. Ask how they found the copy, who they reported it to, how long it stayed up, and who inside the company chased it. Those answers are the price nobody publishes; see [Willing to pay](#willing-to-pay).
3. Offer a Czech law firm that already sells copycat work a partnership. They bring the clients and the letters, you bring the watching and the filing they do by hand today, as [Market gap](#competition) shows.
4. Reach the shops through their trade association rather than one by one. Czech retailers have already joined a common campaign against people impersonating their brands, as [Why now](#why-now) shows, so the room where they compare notes exists already.
