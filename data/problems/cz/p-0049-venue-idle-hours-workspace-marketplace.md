Czech cafés and their laptop guests are stuck: the table is held for hours, and those hours are never sold [S4,S5].

- A café owner says one coffee can buy 5 hours of a table [S4].
- Another café says laptop guests take the seats lunch customers need [S4].
- A freelancer was told to shut her laptop after half an hour [S5].

Four Prague cafés described the same weekday shape: people work from mid-morning until about three, and the room turns social after that [S4].

- One of those cafés switched its wifi off, and a later owner switched it back on to fill the quiet daytime hours with working guests [S4].
- The guest has no way to pay for the chair, and the café has no way to charge for it, so both sides argue about a coffee instead [S4,S5].
- Czech coworking answers the same need in rooms of its own, and a catalogue of it counts 75 spaces in 35 towns [S7].
- A café is open in the morning either way: the rent, the lights and the staff are paid whether the tables fill or not [S4].

Existing non-solutions: Czech sellers of work space sell their own rooms by the hour, the day or the month, and none pools other venues [S7,S10].

Each firm's name, dates and customers are in its row. What they sell:

- An established Prague operator runs six branches and sells day entry, shared desks and dedicated desks in its own rooms [S10].
- An international chain sells access by the hour through an app, again at its own centres [S10].
- A Czech booking product lets an employer reserve the desks and meeting rooms it already owns, so no money passes between strangers [S10].
- A Czech catalogue lists coworking spaces and takes no bookings; it tells visitors to agree the terms with the operator [S7].
- A Prague café runs a small work room about a hundred metres away and sells it by the started hour, one venue and one room at a time [S10].
- Meeting rooms in Prague are sold by the started hour too, by the firms that own the rooms [S10].
- Software vendors sell coworking operators the booking screen, so the operator still holds the space [S10].

Nobody sells the quiet hours of a venue they do not run themselves, and nobody offers a guest a seat in a café down the street the way they offer a desk in their own [S10].

Why now: No law forces this; what has changed is that some cafés now switch the wifi off or ask guests to stop [S4,S5].

- A café that pulls its wifi loses the guests who came to work [S4].
- A guest told to shut her laptop loses the seat and the afternoon [S5].
- A venue with a quiet morning earns nothing from the empty tables [S4].

Nothing in Czech law requires or forbids selling a seat this way, and no dated duty falls on a café, a hotel or the person working there. What is dated is the argument itself:

- In February 2020 four Prague cafés described the working guest and what he costs them, and one had already removed the wifi [S4].
- In February 2025 a Czech columnist wrote about being refused a laptop for half an hour and being told the takings were at risk [S5].
- In April 2025 the Czech press reported German cafés restricting laptops, quoted owners in Cologne and Hamburg, and asked whether the rules were coming here; it found no Czech café that had done it [S6].

Who pays: People who work away from an office already pay Czech venues by the hour, by the day and by the month [S7,S10].

- A Prague café sells hours in its own work room [S10].
- Prague coworking operators sell day entry and monthly shared desks [S10].
- Meeting rooms in the city are sold by the started hour [S10].

What each of them asks is in the prices below. Both are published rates rather than a signed deal, which is what the register can see: private firms do not publish what they pay. No Czech venue is paying anyone for a booking channel of this kind today, so a fee would have to come out of what the working guest already spends, and no Czech figure sets that fee yet [S10].

Solved elsewhere: Marketplaces that sell other people's spare space are established in the United States and Germany [S2,S3].

An American marketplace founded in 2010 lets people book desks, private offices and meeting rooms by the hour or the day, listed by the hosts who own them, hotels among them [S2]. It raised a $14M Series C in October 2014, by which point it had passed 1 million transactions across 5,500 vetted workspaces in 600 cities, and said its supply of hotel workspace had grown 1,060% in a year [S2]. Its own site now offers more than 100,000 desks, offices and meeting rooms in more than 3,500 cities [S2].

A German marketplace trading since July 2015 lets ordinary firms — agencies, startups and small companies — put up the part of their office they do not use, and its site counts 3,235 active listings across 238 German cities [S3]. A travel group invested in it in 2022 [S3]. It brokers offices by the month more often than tables by the hour, so the mechanism is proven there, not this exact product [S3].

The café, hotel and gym version is young. A Portuguese company launched it in January 2026 and raised 350,000 euros that September, after its founders spoke with about 250 café and restaurant owners; one partner hotel took nearly 2,000 euros in a single month [S1]. No Czech example is on file [S10].

## First moves

1. Build a booking page for one café that sells its quiet morning as work seats, and run it for a month. Pick a venue that already lets people work and that empties out between breakfast and lunch; the argument about who may sit where is in [Why now](#why-now). Give the owner a printed rate and one simple rule for how long a seat is held, take the bookings by hand at first, and see whether a guest will pay for the chair instead of buying another coffee.
2. Call the owners who switched their wifi off and ask what would make a working guest welcome again. They are the ones who have already counted the cost, and they said so in public; see [Why now](#why-now). Their answer sets your price, because what you charge has to sit under what the same person pays for a coworking membership today, which is in [Willing to pay](#willing-to-pay).
3. Sign venues one street at a time, so the first guests always find a seat within a short walk. A guest opens an app that has somewhere nearby and closes one that does not, and a venue that never gets a booking takes its listing down; that cold start is the real gate, as [Execution difficulty](#execution-difficulty) says. A single district that works beats a thin map of the whole country.
4. Ask the coworking operators who already sell day entry whether they would list their empty hours too. They sell their own rooms and nothing else today, which is what [Market gap](#competition) sets out, and their quiet stretches cost them the same rent a café's do. They also know what an hour of a desk is worth, which is more than most café owners do.
